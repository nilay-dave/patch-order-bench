    Limitations¶

    Read this before quoting any number from this project.

    It is a simulation, end to end¶

    No figure here was measured against a real vulnerability backlog. The corpus is generated, the remediation programme is a model of capacity spent per week, and the live feed path has never run against the real feeds. docs/sources.md lists the single distributional parameter that has published provenance; everything else is a declared choice.

    What this project can legitimately claim is that under a stated and swept set of assumptions, these orderings produce these outcomes, and that the code producing that claim is in the repository and reruns in under a minute. It cannot claim that a real programme would see these numbers.

    The headline metric is partly a tautology¶

    KEV coverage measures what share of the known exploited findings got remediated, and the winning orderings are the ones that sort by the KEV flag. Of course they win. A benchmark where the metric is the sort key is measuring its own definition.

    This is why the comparison is not reported on KEV coverage alone. Window attainment and exposure removed are scored in the same table, no ordering leads on all three, and the orderings that dominate KEV coverage are near the bottom on windows: kev-then-epss attains

    0.084 against window-first at

    0.649. The useful finding is the tension, not the win.

    The one place KEV coverage is informative on its own is effort: kev-then-epss reaches 90 percent of the known exploited findings after

    121 remediations out of a budget of

    1,040, which says the catalogue is small enough to clear early rather than that sorting by it works.

    Twelve seeds is not many¶

    The spread column in docs/results.md is the range across

    12 seeds, and for the severity ordering it is 0.645, which is wider than most of the differences the table reports. Twelve seeds gives a mean with real uncertainty around it, not a settled figure.

    The practical consequence: the orderings that score 1.000 with a spread of 0.000 are robust, and the ones in the middle of the table should not be ranked against each other on these runs. Raise --seeds before drawing a conclusion from a small gap. The project publishes the spread rather than a confidence interval because a parametric interval on twelve samples of an unknown distribution would be more precise looking and no more honest.

    The corpus generator is not a model of a real backlog¶

    It produces findings whose EPSS distribution is fitted to one published share and whose severity correlates with exploitation probability at a chosen strength. Real backlogs have structure this does not reproduce: findings cluster by asset and by software component, so remediating one often remediates many; ages are not uniform; scanner duplicates and false positives consume capacity; and exploitation probability is not independent across a fleet running the same stack.

    The clustering omission matters most, and it cuts against this project's own conclusion. If one patch clears forty findings, an ordering that groups by component may beat any ordering that ranks findings individually, and no ordering in this tool groups by component.

    Capacity is modelled as a single fungible number¶

    One remediation is one unit of effort. In practice a kernel upgrade on a database cluster and a browser update are not the same unit, change windows are not evenly spaced, and some remediations are blocked on a vendor rather than on capacity. A model where effort is fungible flatters every ordering equally, so it probably does not change the ranking, but it does make the absolute attainment figures optimistic.

    EPSS is a thirty day probability and the simulation is not¶

    EPSS predicts exploitation in the next thirty days. This simulation spends capacity over 26 weeks and scores a single EPSS value per CVE throughout, as though the probability were static. Real EPSS scores move, sometimes sharply, when exploitation is observed. A programme rereading the feed weekly would reorder its backlog as it went, which is strictly more information than any ordering here uses.

    There is no notion of risk accepted or compensating controls¶

    Every finding in the corpus is remediable and worth remediating. Real programmes carry findings behind a WAF rule, on a segmented network, or formally risk accepted, and those are not failures of prioritisation.

    What would make this stronger¶

    In roughly descending order of how much it would change the conclusions:

    1. Run it against a real anonymised backlog, even one.
    2. Add component clustering to the generator, and a grouping ordering to compete.
    3. Replay historical EPSS and KEV snapshots so the scores move during the simulation instead of standing still.
    4. Model effort as a distribution rather than a unit.
    5. More seeds.
